Adaptoid Reverse Engineering                                 Analysis Note
wishk201.sys                                                 2001 driver


                        KNOWN DEFECTS


Abstract

   Fourteen defects have been found during reverse engineering: two
   arithmetic ones in wishk201.sys, a pair of unbounded string copies also
   in the driver, a race in wishh201.dll, a bounds check in the script
   interpreter that tests the wrong value, four in the script engine's
   native builtin library, three on the private IOCTL surface, and two
   remove-lock references that error paths never give back.

   THE TWO REMOVE-LOCK DEFECTS, SECTIONS 16 AND 17, ARE THE SAME MISTAKE
   TWICE and are worth reading together. Both are a reference taken and not
   returned on a path that fails, and in both the consequence is the same: a
   count that never reaches zero, so the device can never finish removing.
   Section 17 is the reachable one. This
   note records each one, the exact condition that triggers it, what it
   would actually do, and whether a user would be likely to notice. It also
   records claims that were made here and later RETRACTED, in sections 3
   and 7.

   TWO OF THEM, SECTIONS 13 AND 14, ARE DIFFERENT IN KIND from the rest.
   Every other entry here is a correctness slip whose worst outcome is
   wrong behaviour. Those two are memory-safety failures on paths that
   reach the driver from an ordinary user-mode request, and section 14 is
   the more serious of the pair because it WRITES. A replacement must not
   reproduce either.

   The purpose is not to criticise a driver that shipped in 2001 and
   worked. It is to stop a clean-room replacement from faithfully
   reproducing a slip, and to record which observed behaviour is
   deliberate and which is accidental.

   The first five are in paths a normal session does not reach. The four
   in the builtin library, sections 10 to 13, are different: they sit in
   _fork, _kill and _wake, which a concurrent script uses deliberately,
   and two of them are reachable from an ordinary compiled script rather
   than only from a hand-built one. They are still not the sort of thing a
   simple remapping profile would hit, which is why the software's
   reputation for robustness survives them.

   None of the fourteen is in the HID report descriptor or the keyboard and
   mouse report builders.

2.  Defect 1: Button Map Filler Entries

   Location:  drv_BuildJoystickReport, the button loop at 000153a3
              drv_ButtonMap at 00019d80

   drv_ButtonMap has 16 entries, one per bit of the N64 button word, but
   only 14 of those bits are buttons. Bit positions 8 and 9 are Reset and
   an unused bit, and their table entries hold 0.

   The loop does not special-case them. It executes

       mask |= 1 << drv_ButtonMap[index]

   unconditionally for all 16 indices, so an index holding the filler
   value 0 asserts HID button 1 - the same bit that A uses.

   Trigger:  the N64 controller asserts its Reset bit when L + R + Start
             are pressed together. That is the only realistic way bit 8
             becomes set, and it is confirmed by the configurator, which
             names the bit 8 script handler on_lrs - L, R, Start.

   Effect:   while L + R + Start is held, the gamepad reports button 1
             as pressed even though A is not.

   Would it be noticed? Rarely. L + R + Start is the controller reset
   combination, not something pressed during play. It also overlaps the
   on-controller tuning combination L + R + Z + Start, during which a
   spurious A press is unlikely to be observed.

   For a replacement: treat a map entry of 0 at indices 8 and 9 as "no
   button", or better, give the table a sentinel such as 0xFF for "not a
   button" and test for it.

3.  RETRACTED: Cross-Coupled Stick Acceleration Is Not A Defect

   An earlier revision of this note recorded the cross-coupled stick term in
   drv_BuildJoystickReport as an arithmetic defect. THAT WAS WRONG, and this
   section records the correction rather than deleting the claim.

   The term is applied as

       X += (|Y| * k * X) / 256
       Y += (|X| * k * Y) / 256

   with k taken from devext+0x3F0, which drv_AddDevice initialises to 10. The
   earlier reading was that a conventional acceleration curve would use each
   axis's own magnitude, so cross-coupling looked like a transposition slip.

   It is not acceleration. The vendor SDK, shipped as sdk_1163b.zip and
   documented in src/dxsample/dxescape.cpp, names these two parameters and
   describes exactly this transform:

       0x7834BB10   joystick clipping range   default 75   devext+0x3EC
       0x7834BB11   joystick corner stretch   default 10   devext+0x3F0

   and says of the second, verbatim:

       "Corner stretching is used by the Adaptoid driver to map the octagonal
       range of the analog stick to the square range expected by PC games.
       [...] The default value of 10 warps the maximum deflection of 65 out
       to 75 in the corners, matching the default range clipping of 75 in the
       horizontal and vertical directions. [...] Note that the warping is
       smooth and linear across the full range."

   The N64 stick's gate is octagonal. It reaches about 80 raw units on an
   axis but only about 65 into a diagonal corner, so a diagonal can never
   reach full scale. A term that is ZERO on-axis and MAXIMAL on the diagonal
   is precisely the correction needed, and cross-coupling is the only way to
   express it. Using each axis's own magnitude - the "fix" the earlier
   revision proposed - would have boosted the axes that already reach full
   scale and done nothing for the corners, which is the opposite of what is
   wanted.

   The arithmetic in the earlier revision proves the point once it is read
   correctly. Reports are raw * 16, clipped at 1200, so 1200/16 = 75 is the
   clipping range. At the diagonal maximum of 65 raw on each axis:

       base  = 65 * 16                 = 1040
       boost = (65 * 10 * 65) / 256    =  165
       final = 1040 + 165             = 1205, clipped to 1200

   1200 is full scale. The corner reaches it exactly, which is the documented
   behaviour: 65 warped out to 75. The earlier note recorded the same number
   and described the clip as "hiding the error", when reaching full diagonal
   deflection was the entire purpose.

   For a replacement: implement this deliberately, and expose both
   parameters. Setting corner stretch to 0 disables the warp and setting
   range clipping to 128 restores the controller's raw range, both documented
   by the SDK. Neither is a bug to be fixed.

   LESSON, recorded because it generalises: the cross-coupling looked wrong
   against an assumed intent (acceleration) that the code never claimed. The
   driver alone did not name the parameters. It was only the vendor's own
   documentation that supplied the intent, and with the right intent the code
   is correct. Where a routine's PURPOSE is inferred rather than read, a
   "defect" in it is only as sound as that inference.

4.  Defect 3: Swapped Sine Interpolation Weights

   Location:  drv_SineLerp at 000149a0, verified in the disassembly at
              000149c2 through 000149df

   The routine interpolates drv_SineTable, whose entries are
   1000 * sin(i * 5 degrees). Linear interpolation should pair
   table[idx] with (500 - rem) and table[idx+1] with rem. The code does
   the reverse:

       000149c2  MOVSX ECX, word ptr [ESI*2 + 0x19d02]   ; table[idx+1]
       000149cf  SUB   EAX, EDX                          ; 500 - rem
       000149d1  IMUL  ECX, EAX                          ; wrong pairing
       000149d4  MOVSX EAX, word ptr [ESI*2 + 0x19d00]   ; table[idx]
       000149dc  IMUL  EAX, EDX

   Within every 5-degree cell the output therefore runs backwards:

       angle (hundredths of a degree)    0    250    499    500
       returned                        870    435      1   1740
       correct                           0    435    869    871

   The result is a rising sawtooth rather than a sine. It is correct only
   where two adjacent table entries are equal, which happens at the top
   of the quarter wave.

   Trigger:  any running effect slot, since drv_EffectEvaluate calls this
             for every evaluation.

   Effect:   whatever the effect engine drives receives a sawtooth with a
             20-step discontinuity per quarter cycle instead of a smooth
             sine.

   Would it be noticed? Only by someone using the effect engine. If, as
   inferred, the engine drives Rumble Pak feedback through duty-cycle
   modulation, the result would feel buzzy or uneven rather than smooth -
   easily attributed to the Rumble Pak itself rather than to the driver.
   It has no effect on scripts, remapping, or any HID report.

   For a replacement: implement the interpolation correctly. Nothing
   should depend on the sawtooth.

5.  Defect 4: Race On The Launch-Detection Path Buffer

   Location:  wishh201.dll hook_ShellProc at 10001000
              the shared buffer hook_g_szLastModulePath at 10005008

   On HSHELL_WINDOWCREATED the hook posts its notification to the
   configurator FIRST, and only then writes the launching process's image
   path into a buffer in the DLL's shared .sdata section. There is exactly
   one such buffer for the whole system, and the configurator reads it
   asynchronously through GetInfo when it gets round to processing message
   0x47B.

   Trigger: two processes creating their first window close enough together
   that the second hook invocation overwrites the buffer before the
   configurator has drained the first message.

   Effect: the configurator reads the wrong image path. It either activates
   the script bound to the wrong game, or finds no match at all and logs
   "Launch detection failed to retrieve...".

   Would it be noticed? Only with DetectGameLaunches enabled, and only when
   two applications start at once - a login, or a game started through a
   launcher process. The visible symptom is a profile that silently fails to
   switch, which a user would retry rather than report.

   Unlike the other three, this one is NOT arithmetic and NOT in the driver.
   It is a design choice in the smallest of the three binaries.

   For a replacement: do not reproduce it. The path belongs in the message,
   not beside it - posting a copy, or using WM_COPYDATA, removes the shared
   buffer entirely. Better still, a modern replacement should not use a
   global WH_SHELL hook at all. A WMI __InstanceCreationEvent subscription on
   Win32_Process, or an ETW process-start session, delivers the image path
   atomically with the event and needs no DLL injected into every process on
   the machine.

6.  Defect 5: Unbounded String Copies Into Fixed Fields

   Location:  drv_RegisterDeviceInterface at 000190a0
              drv_SetDeviceNameAndSort at 00011e02 via drv_StrCopy

   Two places copy a string into a fixed-size field of the device extension
   with no bound on the destination.

   drv_RegisterDeviceInterface copies the symbolic link name returned by
   IoRegisterDeviceInterface into DevExt->InterfaceName. The loop is driven
   purely by the source Length - dwords, then a 0 to 3 byte tail - and is
   never compared against the destination size. The field immediately after
   it is SystemPowerState.

   drv_SetDeviceNameAndSort is worse in shape. It builds a display name and
   writes it through drv_StrCopy, which is a plain strcpy with no length
   parameter at all. The destination, DevExt->DeviceName, is TEN BYTES. At
   offset +10 sits FirmwareRevision, and at +16 GlobalDeviceLink, the
   LIST_ENTRY that threads this device onto the driver-wide list walked by
   drv_SetDeviceNameAndSort itself and by drv_DispatchPnp.

   Trigger:   an interface name longer than 0x200 bytes, or a device
              location name of ten characters or more.

   Effect: the first overruns into a power-state field. The second overruns
   into a firmware value and then into kernel list pointers, which is a far
   more serious target.

   Would it be noticed? Almost certainly not, and neither is reachable by an
   attacker. The names are produced by the system and by this driver, not by
   user input. A device interface name for this device runs to roughly a
   hundred bytes against a 0x200 field. The location name is a controller
   letter followed by a short hub path, well inside ten characters.

   One accident limits the second: drv_SetDeviceNameAndSort reads
   FirmwareRevision BEFORE it writes the name, so the value it goes on to use
   cannot be corrupted by its own copy.

   Note also that the SIZE of InterfaceName is itself an inference - 0x200 is
   the distance to the next field in use, not a figure stated anywhere in the
   code. The DeviceName size is firmer, being the distance to a field that is
   genuinely read.

   For a replacement: bound both copies. This costs nothing and removes a
   whole class of question.

7.  RETRACTED: The Script State Field Is Not A Latch Bug

   An earlier revision of this note, and of ioctl-surface.txt, recorded the
   field at devext+0x1640 as a counter with a latch defect: drv_AddDevice
   initialises it to -1, and drv_ScriptSchedulerDpc only increments while it
   is non-negative, so it appeared that nothing could ever lift it and the
   script could never own the stick.

   THAT WAS WRONG. The field is a state, not a counter, and IOCTL fn 0x83C
   writes its distinguished values:

       -1   no script loaded          -2   a load is in progress
        0   script loaded and idle    -3   also accepted by the load
       >0   scheduler passes running       path

   So -1 is the correct initial state and the one-sided guards are refusing
   to count scheduler passes while the engine is unloaded or loading. A load
   through fn 0x83C moves the field to 0 and the mechanism works.

   LESSON, the same one as section 3: the behaviour looked wrong against an
   assumed purpose the code never claimed. Naming the field
   ScriptStickCounter committed to the counter reading before the IOCTL that
   drives it had been read. Where a field's PURPOSE is inferred from one of
   its users, a defect in it is only as sound as that inference.

8.  Defect 6: The Immediate Call Bounds-Checks The Wrong Value

   Location:  drv_ScriptExecute, the 0x181 handler at 00017ff2, and in
              particular the compare at 00017ffc
   Compare:   the 0x080 handler at 00017e3c, compare at 00017e46

   Both call opcodes resolve a tagged target and, when the tag says the
   target is a script function (0x10000000), range-check the resulting
   word index against the code length before jumping. The indirect form,
   opcode 0x080, checks the value it is about to jump to:

       target = acc & 0x0fffffff
       if (target < CodeCount)  { push return address; PC = target }
       else                       status = 6

   The immediate form, opcode 0x181, takes its target from the operand
   word that follows the opcode but performs the comparison on the
   ACCUMULATOR:

       target = operand & 0x0fffffff
       if (acc < CodeCount)     { push return address; PC = target }
       else                       status = 6

   The two handlers are otherwise the same shape, which is what makes the
   slip visible. In the disassembly the accumulator lives in ECX for the
   whole dispatch loop:

       0x080 at 00017e3c        0x181 at 00017ff2
         MOV EAX,[ESP+0x34]       MOV EAX,[ESP+0x34]    ; CodeCount
         AND ECX,0xfffffff        AND EDX,0xfffffff     ; the target
         CMP ECX,EAX              CMP ECX,EAX           ; <-- ECX not EDX
         JL  taken                MOV [ESP+0x18],EDX
                                  JL  taken

   The accumulator at that point is whatever the previous instruction
   left behind, and it has no relation to the call target. The comparison
   is therefore not a bounds check at all: it passes or fails on an
   unrelated value.

   Trigger:  a direct call whose target word index is >= CodeCount, made
   at a point where the accumulator happens to be less than CodeCount.
   Scripts are compiled rather than hand-written and the configurator
   emits only targets it has itself laid out, so a target past the end of
   the code does not arise from a valid compile. Reaching it needs either
   a corrupted code array or a hand-built one delivered through IOCTL
   0xb98c20f0, which section 8 of script-bytecode.txt already notes is
   accepted without a verification pass.

   Effect:  PC is set past the end of the code array. The next iteration
   of the interpreter loop re-tests PC against the code end at 00017c6a
   and stops the thread with status 6, so no word outside the array is
   ever fetched. A negative index cannot occur: the tag mask leaves 28
   unsigned bits, so the largest possible target is 0x0fffffff.

   Would a user notice:  no. The failure this check exists to catch is
   caught one instruction later by the loop's own PC test, with the same
   status code. The defect costs one wasted dispatch and nothing else.
   It is recorded because it is a real slip, not because it has
   consequences.

   In the replacement:  NOT reproduced. core_script_run in
   src_drv/script.c checks the target for both opcodes. The divergence is
   deliberate and is commented at the point it occurs, because
   reproducing it would mean writing code that is wrong on purpose to
   preserve behaviour that cannot be observed.

9.  Defect 7: _fork Gives The Child The Parent's StackSize

   Location:  drv_ScriptNativeCall, the _fork case, the copy at 00018a15

   _fork allocates the child with drv_ScriptThreadAlloc(DevExt, depth),
   where depth is the parent's CURRENT stack depth, and then copies the
   whole 0x24-byte header from parent to child with one rep movsd:

       00018a09  MOV EDX,[EAX+0x8]     ; save the child's own ThreadId
       00018a0c  MOV ECX,0x9
       00018a15  MOVSD.REP             ; 9 dwords = the entire header
       00018a2c  MOV [EAX+0x8],EDX     ; restore ThreadId
       00018a2f  MOV [EAX+0x10],0      ; SavedAcc = 0

   Nine dwords is Link, ThreadId, Pc, SavedAcc, SavedDepth, WakeTime and
   StackSize. Only ThreadId and SavedAcc are put back, so the child keeps
   the PARENT'S StackSize while owning a node sized for the parent's
   depth.

   Trigger:  any fork by a thread whose StackSize exceeds its current
   depth. StackSize is a high-water mark - drv_ScriptExecute raises it
   whenever the stack outgrows the node and never lowers it - so a thread
   that once ran deep and has since returned to a shallow frame is exactly
   the case. Nothing exotic is needed: a script that calls a few levels
   deep and then forks from the top level does it.

   Effect:  the next time the child runs, the restore loop at 00017c10
   copies StackSize words out of a node that holds far fewer, reading up
   to (200 - 8) * 4 = 768 bytes of pool memory past the end of the
   allocation. Those words land in the shared local region, where the
   script can read them back as locals. It is an over-read only; the save
   path writes just SavedDepth words, so nothing is corrupted.

   Would a user notice:  not directly. Nothing crashes, because the read
   stays inside the pool, and the junk sits in locals the script has not
   initialised. It is a genuine out-of-bounds read and an information
   leak into a script, not a visible malfunction.

   In the replacement:  NOT reproduced. core_sched native_fork in
   src_drv/sched.c copies the fields one at a time and the child keeps the
   stack_size of the node it was actually given.

10. Defect 8: _kill Never Decrements ThreadCount

   Location:  drv_ScriptNativeCall, the _kill case

   ThreadCount at devext+0x1658 is written in exactly three places, and a
   search of the whole image confirms it: drv_ScriptLoad zeroes it,
   drv_ScriptThreadQueue increments it, and drv_ScriptScheduler decrements
   it when it takes a thread off the list to run. drv_ScriptNativeCall only
   ever READS it, at 000189e1.

   So _kill, which unlinks its victim from ReadyThreadList and pushes the
   node onto FreeThreadList, leaves the count as if the thread were still
   queued.

   Trigger:  any _kill of another thread. Killing yourself falls through
   into _exit and does not take this path.

   Effect:  the count drifts upward by one per kill and never recovers.
   _fork refuses once it exceeds 29:

       000189e1  CMP dword ptr [EBP+0x1658], 0x1e
       000189e8  JGE refuse

   so after thirty kills _fork returns -1 for the rest of the script's
   life, whatever the real thread count is. Only reloading the script
   clears it, because that is the one place the count is zeroed.

   Would a user notice:  yes, eventually, in a script that spawns and
   kills helper threads in a loop - a hold-to-autofire pattern is exactly
   that shape. The symptom is _fork quietly returning -1 and the script
   losing its concurrency, with no error and no event.

   In the replacement:  NOT reproduced. The count is decremented.

11. Defect 9: _wake Leaves The Ready List Unsorted

   Location:  drv_ScriptNativeCall, the _wake case

   ReadyThreadList is kept sorted by WakeTime, which is what lets
   drv_ScriptScheduler stop its sweep at the first thread that is not yet
   due. _wake finds its target by walking that list and then writes the
   new wake time into the node IN PLACE:

       piVar9[6] = Thread->WakeTimeLow
       piVar9[7] = Thread->WakeTimeHigh
       piVar9[4] = 0                      ; SavedAcc, so the sleeper
                                          ; learns it was woken early

   The node is not moved. Since the target was asleep its old wake time
   was later than the caller's, so after the write the list is out of
   order.

   Trigger:  _wake on a thread that is not the earliest sleeper.

   Effect:  the woken thread sits behind a thread that is still not due,
   and the sweep stops before reaching it. It does not run at the woken
   time; it runs when the thread ahead of it becomes due. With

       A asleep until 5000, B asleep until 9000, now 2000

   waking B sets its time to 2000 but leaves it behind A, so B first runs
   at 5000 - three seconds late. The timer is armed from the head, so
   nothing corrects it earlier.

   Would a user notice:  yes, as a script that responds late or appears to
   miss a wake, and intermittently, since it depends on what else happens
   to be sleeping. This is the most likely of the four to be seen in
   practice and the hardest to attribute.

   In the replacement:  NOT reproduced. The thread is unlinked and
   requeued, so the list stays sorted.

12. Defect 10: The Native Argument Frame Is One Word Short

   Location:  drv_ScriptNativeCall, every case that reads ArgFrame[2]
   Related:   the Vars allocation in drv_ScriptLoad

   A native reads argument k from stack slot sp + k. Vars is allocated as
   VarCount * 4 + 0x328, and 0x328 is 808 where the 200 locals need only
   800, so the eight spare bytes are two extra words. That is not padding:
   it is exactly the headroom a two-argument call needs at depth 199,
   which is the deepest a script can address, since core index checking
   rejects a local above 199 and opcode 0x192 therefore cannot name slot
   200 or beyond.

   It is one word short. The interpreter faults a depth only ABOVE 200, so
   a script can reach depth 200 exactly, and a two-argument builtin called
   there reads argument 2 from slot 202 - four bytes past the end of the
   allocation.

   Trigger:  a script that pushes to depth 200 and then calls a
   two-argument builtin. The compiler does not emit that, so it needs a
   hand-built download through IOCTL 0xb98c20f0.

   Effect:  four bytes of adjacent pool are read and used as an argument.
   Every consumer either clamps it, masks it, or passes it out as an event
   argument, so the value is disclosed rather than acted on dangerously.
   Nothing is written out of bounds.

   Would a user notice:  no. It is not reachable from a compiled script,
   and the argument could not have been written by the script anyway.

   In the replacement:  NOT reproduced. native_arg bounds-checks the slot
   and returns zero, which is indistinguishable from the original for any
   argument a script could actually have set.

13. Defect 11: The N64 Transaction Receive Length Is Unbounded

   Location:  drv_N64Transaction, the reply buffer and the short-form
              transfer at 000138d8
   Reached by: IOCTL 0xb98c20e8, function 0x83a

   THIS IS THE ONE MEMORY-SAFETY DEFECT IN THE DRIVER. Everything else in
   this note is a correctness slip; this one is a missing bounds check on a
   length that comes from user mode.

   The chain, verified end to end:

   1.  drv_IoctlViaHidHandle (00012710) passes the IRP's
       InputBufferLength and OutputBufferLength straight through as the
       TxLen and RxLen arguments of drv_IoctlDeviceCommand. Neither is
       inspected on the way.

   2.  IOCTL 0xb98c20e8 dispatches to the case at 00012df3. Confirmed
       through the jump table: 0xb98c20e8 + 0x4673df3c truncates to 0x24,
       the index byte at 0x13664 is 8, and the entry at 0x13610 is
       00012df3. That case tests only that both lengths are NON-ZERO and
       then calls drv_N64Transaction.

   3.  drv_N64Transaction's reply buffer is SIXTY-FOUR BYTES OF STACK -
       one status byte plus a 63-byte payload. Its only length guard is
       (TxLen < 5 || RxLen < 4), which tests whether RxLen is too SMALL.
       There is no upper bound anywhere on the path.

   4.  The short form then issues a vendor IN transfer of RxLen + 1 bytes
       into that buffer, and on a non-zero first byte copies RxLen bytes
       back out of it into the output buffer the caller receives.

   Trigger:  a device-control request on this function code with a small
   input length, so that the short form is selected, and an output length
   larger than 63.

   Effect:  two distinct problems, from the same missing check.

       The copy back out reads past the 64-byte buffer and hands the
       caller whatever follows it on the kernel stack. This needs no
       cooperation from the hardware; the bytes are disclosed whether or
       not the device answered with that many.

       The transfer itself is set up to receive more bytes than the buffer
       holds. How much is actually written depends on what the device
       sends, so the genuine adapter will not overflow it - but the length
       the driver promises the USB stack is attacker-chosen, and nothing
       between the request and the stack buffer narrows it.

   Would a user notice:  no, which is the point. Nothing misbehaves during
   normal use, because every shipped caller passes a constant. Three of the
   four call sites pass 3, 33 and 1 literally; only the passthrough takes
   the length from the request.

   In the replacement:  NOT reproduced, and this is the one divergence in
   the port that is not optional. core_n64_transaction in src_drv/core.c
   refuses any rx_len above CORE_N64_RX_MAX, which is 63 - the largest
   reply the original's own buffer could ever have held. The harness
   verifies the refusal, and removing that check makes the harness itself
   die on the spot with a smashed stack, which is the most direct
   demonstration of the defect available without a live driver.

   A replacement should ALSO bound the length where the request is parsed,
   rather than relying only on the helper. Defence in one place is how this
   happened.

14. Defect 12: The Effect Slot Index Is Signed And Only Bounded Above

   Location:  drv_IoctlDeviceCommand, the fn 0x853 case at 0001320b and the
              fn 0x855 case at 000132ed
   Also:      drv_EffectSlotActive (00013e90), which checks nothing at all

   THIS IS THE MORE SERIOUS OF THE TWO MEMORY-SAFETY DEFECTS, because it
   writes. Defect 11 discloses memory; this one corrupts it.

   Function 0x853 takes a two-byte input, {command, slot}. The slot byte is
   loaded as a SIGNED char and compared against 32 with a SIGNED branch:

       00013224  MOV   AL, byte ptr [EDI + 0x1]     ; the slot byte
       00013227  CMP   AL, 0x20
       00013229  JGE   reject                       ; signed - upper bound only
       ...
       0001323a  MOVSX EAX, AL                      ; sign-extended
       0001323f  SHL   EDX, 0x3
       00013242  SUB   EDX, EAX                     ; EDX = slot * 7
       00013244  MOV   ECX, [ESI + EDX*8 + 0x46c]   ; devext + slot*0x38 + ...

   Every byte from 0x80 to 0xFF is negative as a signed char, so every one
   of them passes a test meant to keep the index inside a 32-entry array.
   The effective address is devext + slot * 0x38 + field, and with the slot
   at -128 that is 0x1C00 bytes BEFORE the device extension.

   Trigger:  a device-control request on function 0x853 with a slot byte of
   0x80 or above. Nothing else is needed - no timing, no particular device
   state, and the input is two bytes.

   Effect:  three dwords are written at a caller-chosen negative offset.
   Command 1 writes Running and the two halves of the start timestamp:

       00013256  MOV dword ptr [EAX + 0x44c], 0x1
       0001327f  MOV dword ptr [ESI + EDI*8 + 0x450], EAX
       00013286  MOV dword ptr [ESI + EDI*8 + 0x454], EDX

   and is gated on a dword read from that same out-of-bounds region being
   positive. COMMAND 2 HAS NO GATE AT ALL: it writes a zero dword at the
   chosen offset unconditionally. The offset moves in 0x38-byte steps over
   a 0x1C00-byte window, so this is a controlled write into whatever the
   allocator placed before the device extension.

   Function 0x855 is the same slip on the read side. Its one-byte input is
   loaded the same way and handed to drv_EffectSlotActive, which indexes
   EffectSlot[SlotIndex] with no bounds check whatsoever - and which also
   WRITES Running on its expiry path, so it is not purely a read.

   Would a user notice:  no. The configurator only ever sends slot numbers
   it chose itself, which are 0 to 31, so nothing misbehaves in normal use.

   In the replacement:  NOT reproduced. core_ioctl_dispatch in
   src_drv/ioctl.c rejects any slot outside 0..31 on both paths, and
   core_effect_slot_active bounds-checks its own argument as well rather
   than trusting its caller - which is precisely the trust that failed
   here. The harness checks five representative negative bytes against both
   commands and the query.

15. Defect 13: The Script Fault Snapshot Can Leak

   Location:  drv_IoctlDeviceCommand, the fn 0x83d case at 0001333b

   Draining the post-mortem dump takes BOTH buffers out of the device
   extension under the script lock - the faulted thread node and a snapshot
   of the globals - and then copies out as much as the caller's buffer
   holds. The thread node is freed unconditionally. The snapshot is not:

       uVar14 = OutputLength - uVar16;        ; room left after the thread
       if (0 < (int)uVar14) {
           ... copy the globals ...
           ExFreePool(vars);                  ; INSIDE the branch
       }

   so when the output buffer is exactly the size of the thread node, or
   smaller, the snapshot is never released. It has already been cleared
   from devext+0x1688, so nothing else can ever find it either.

   Trigger:  drain a fault record with an output buffer that leaves no room
   for the globals. A caller sizing its buffer from the thread node alone
   does this every time.

   Effect:  one pool allocation of VarCount * 4 bytes leaks per drained
   fault. Small, but unbounded over a long session with a misbehaving
   script, and driver pool is never reclaimed until unload.

   There is a second, narrower path: if the thread pointer is null the
   function returns immediately without looking at the snapshot pointer. In
   the shipped driver the two are always set together, so this arm is not
   reachable, but a port that keeps the structure should not rely on that.

   Would a user notice:  not directly. It needs a faulting script and a
   caller that sizes its buffer tightly, and the symptom is pool usage that
   does not come back.

   In the replacement:  NOT reproduced. Both buffers are released on every
   path in ioc_script_fault, including the null-thread one. The harness
   drains a real fault with an output buffer exactly the size of the thread
   node and asserts that the allocator got both blocks back.

16. Defect 14: A Remove Lock Is Not Released On One Path

   Location:  drv_AcquireControlDeviceExt (000105a7)

   The function takes the calling device's remove lock and then returns the
   control device's extension:

       if (drv_LockAcquire(&DevExt->RemoveLockA, ...) < 0) return NULL;
       if (drv_g_ControlDeviceObject == 0)                 return NULL;
       return ControlDeviceObject->DeviceExtension;

   The two NULL returns are not alike. The first happens before the lock is
   taken and is correct. THE SECOND HAPPENS AFTER, and drops the reference
   on the floor.

   Its name says it acquires, and both callers pair it with
   drv_ReleaseControlDeviceExt only when the result is non-null:

       CdoExt = drv_AcquireControlDeviceExt(DevExt);
       if (CdoExt == NULL) { ...give up...; }          /* no release */
       ... ; drv_ReleaseControlDeviceExt(DevExt);

   so on the second path the lock is never released. There are exactly two
   callers, drv_QueueUserNotification and drv_DisableDeviceInterface, and
   both have that shape.

   Trigger:  posting a user-mode notification, or disabling a device
   interface, at a moment when drv_g_ControlDeviceObject is null. That
   global is cleared by drv_DeleteControlSymlink, which runs BEFORE
   drv_DeleteControlDevice, so the window opens as soon as the control
   device begins tearing down.

   How narrow is it:  narrow. The control device is torn down only when the
   last handle to it closes AND drv_g_ControlDeviceRefCount is zero, and
   that refcount is meant to be non-zero while any adapter exists. It needs
   an adapter to be removing itself concurrently with the last control
   handle closing - which is exactly what unplugging the adapter while the
   configurator exits looks like.

   Effect:  a remove lock that never returns to zero. The device can then
   never complete removal: drv_LockReleaseAndWait waits for a count that
   will not arrive, so the removal IRP blocks indefinitely. The symptom is
   a device stuck in removal, not a crash.

   Would a user notice:  only as a device that will not go away, and only
   after an unlucky ordering. It is recorded because the consequence is
   disproportionate to how small the slip is, and because a port that keeps
   the same helper shape inherits it silently.

   In the replacement:  the shape is avoided rather than patched. Nothing in
   src_drv/ioctl.c couples acquiring a reference to looking something up -
   the registry and the notification queue are plain data structures, and
   whatever the OS layer needs to hold across a call it acquires and
   releases on its own path. A helper that sometimes acquires and sometimes
   does not, distinguished only by its return value, is the defect.

17. Defect 15: A Rejected Vendor Request Acquires Instead Of Releasing

   Location:  drv_SendVendorRequest, the failure path, the call at 00019509

   The function takes RemoveLockB on entry and, on success, holds it across
   the transfer for drv_VendorUrbComplete to drop. On failure it has to give
   it back. It does not:

       00019503  PUSH 0x193d8
       00019508  PUSH EDX
       00019509  CALL 0x00015930          <-- drv_LockAcquire

   drv_LockAcquire is at 00015930. drv_LockRelease is at 00015970. The call
   targets the wrong one, so the failure path takes a SECOND reference
   instead of returning the first.

   The neighbouring function gets it right: drv_SubmitVendorUrb's own
   failure path at 0001951d calls 00015970, which is what makes this read as
   a slip rather than a misunderstanding.

   Trigger:  any call whose setup packet has a bmRequestType other than 0x40
   or 0xC0. AND THAT BYTE IS CALLER-SUPPLIED: the raw vendor passthrough,
   private IOCTL function 0x832, hands the first six bytes of the caller's
   input straight to this function as the setup packet. So one device-control
   request with a single wrong byte is the whole trigger.

   Effect:  two leaked references per rejected request - the one taken on
   entry and the one the bogus call adds. Neither is ever returned, so
   RemoveLockB's count climbs monotonically and never reaches zero. The
   device can then never complete removal: drv_LockReleaseAndWait waits on an
   event that will not be signalled, and the removal IRP blocks
   indefinitely.

   The function also RETURNS THE ACQUIRE'S STATUS rather than the
   STATUS_INVALID_PARAMETER it computed, so a rejected request reports
   success to its caller. The pending IRP is still completed with the right
   error, so user mode sees the failure; only the internal return value is
   wrong.

   Would a user notice:  not at first. The adapter keeps working - nothing
   is corrupted and no transfer is affected. The symptom appears later, as a
   device that will not unplug cleanly, with no apparent connection to the
   request that caused it.

   In the replacement:  NOT reproduced. AdaptoidVendorSend in src_drv/wdm.c
   validates bmRequestType, gives the slot back, releases the reference and
   returns STATUS_INVALID_PARAMETER. The harness rejects six different bad
   values in a row and asserts the count is exactly where it started;
   restoring the original's call makes that count reach thirteen instead of
   one, which is the two-per-rejection arithmetic showing through.

18. What Is Not Affected

   It is worth stating plainly which paths were examined and found free
   of these defects, because the practical reliability of the original
   software depends on them:

   -  The script compiler in wishd201.exe and the bytecode it emits.
   -  The bytecode interpreter drv_ScriptExecute and its
      100000-instruction budget. Its per-instruction PC check is sound;
      the one slip in its bounds checking is defect 6, section 8, and
      that slip is masked by the per-instruction check.
   -  The input, button and stick primitives of the native builtin
      library. Its THREAD primitives are not clean: see defects 7, 8 and
      9, which are all in _fork, _kill and _wake.
   -  The keyboard and mouse report builders drv_HidKeyEvent,
      drv_HidMouseButtonEvent and drv_HidMouseMoveEvent, all of which
      assemble reports that match the descriptor byte for byte.
   -  The HID report descriptors themselves and their selection.
   -  The dispatch wrapper triage. The private IOCTL surface itself is
      NOT clean: function 0x83a reaches defect 11 (section 13), functions
      0x853 and 0x855 are defect 12 (section 14), and function 0x83d is
      defect 13 (section 15). Fifteen of its eighteen function codes are
      sound; the three that are not are all bounds or lifetime slips
      rather than logic errors.
   -  The Controller Pak CRCs, which are the genuine N64 algorithms.

   A user writing complex scripts exercises every item in that list. That
   no longer means they meet none of the defects: the three thread
   primitives in sections 9 to 11 are reached by exactly the kind of
   concurrent script this list was describing.
